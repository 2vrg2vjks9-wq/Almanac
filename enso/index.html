About this collection

Wabi-sabi finds its beauty in what's worn, asymmetrical, and passing — a cracked bowl mended with gold rather than hidden. This library gathers poems that sit close to that feeling: impermanence, plainness, the weather of a life. It leans on poets who wrote in that register long before "wabi-sabi" was an imported word — Emily Dickinson's quiet rooms, Bashō's frogs and ponds, the Rubáiyát's turning cup, Blake's grain of sand.

Every poem here is in the public domain in the United States — mostly work published before 1930, several decades before that. That's a deliberate line, not a taste: it's the only way an app like this can hold full texts without asking permission first.

That's also why you won't find Pablo Neruda, Mary Oliver, or the popular English Rumi translations (Coleman Barks and others) bundled in. Neruda's and Oliver's poems are still under copyright — Oliver died in 2019, and Neruda's estate holds rights into the 2040s. And most of the "Rumi" lines that circulate online are modern paraphrases, not translations of anything Rumi actually wrote, which made honest sourcing hard. Use the Add your own tab to bring in poems like these from books or editions you already have — they'll stay local to your browser, clearly marked as yours.

The Zen poems — Cold Mountain, Wang Wei, Ryōkan, Dōgen, the Platform Sutra verses, and haiku by Bashō, Issa, Buson and others — were written between the 7th and 19th centuries. Most well-known English versions are recent and still under copyright, so these were translated fresh for this collection from the Chinese and Japanese, keeping close to the plain sense of the originals. They're marked “translated for Ensō”; tap the zen theme to see them together.

Texts here are transcribed from memory of standard public-domain editions. For scholarly or exacting use, check them against a primary source — Dickinson's dashes especially vary by editor.
